Who the reading is for. An intelligent reader outside the field who will read
it instead of the source, on a small screen or aloud. Write as a well-edited
article reads: connected paragraphs and transitions that show how one point
bears on the next. Across a passage, let most sentences fall roughly between
twenty and forty words; this is a cadence target, not a sentence-by-sentence
ceiling or quota. Use a short sentence when it sharpens emphasis, but do not
make a run of clipped statements that each restart with an actor and a
reporting verb. Use a longer sentence when a comparison, qualification, or
consequence must remain attached, but split it when it contains two
independent turns or becomes hard to follow. Before returning the JSON,
silently revise in both directions: combine adjacent clipped statements that
belong together, and divide overloaded sentences without detaching their
caveats. Let each paragraph develop one coherent point rather than target a
size. The first paragraph of the complete reading lets a newcomer see what the
subject is and what is being claimed about it. Do not write notes, a list of
claims in sentence form, or a lightly edited copy of the source.

What to keep. Every claim, mechanism, quantity, condition, limitation,
disagreement, and conclusion whose loss would change a reader's understanding
of the material's argument, results, constraints, or conclusions, covered from
beginning to end so that no section eats the budget of the rest. This is a
reading, not a restatement: compress supporting detail, repetition, procedure,
and asides, and keep an example only when it makes a claim understandable. A
qualification attached to a claim stays with that claim: where the material
calls something uncertain, disputed, assumed, correlational, or conditional,
the reading says so in the same sentence. Keep a number with its subject,
unit, range, direction of comparison, and time scope, and never attach a
figure from one passage to another passage's subject. Keep attribution,
chronology, and negation as they are.

What not to add. Nothing the material does not support: no outside background,
no evaluation, no implication it does not draw, no number, date, or name it
does not contain, and no causal or consensus link between facts it merely
places side by side. Explain a term only with the material's own explanation.
Keep a technical term that carries a real distinction rather than swapping it
for a grander word.

How it reads. Write about the subject directly and in the third person, as fact
where the material treats it as fact and as contested where it does not. Do
not describe the document, its author, or its sections, and do not comment on
the reading itself. Start a new paragraph when the topic, actor, or side of a
disagreement changes. A long Detailed reading may carry a few descriptive ##
headings where the material has real divisions; the Brief rarely needs any.
No bullet points, numbered lists, tables, or other list markup: where a set or
sequence matters, name its members in connected prose, over several sentences
if need be. No first person, no citations, no footnotes, and no closing remark.
